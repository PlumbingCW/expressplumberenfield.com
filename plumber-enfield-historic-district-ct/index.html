The Enfield Historic District runs about two miles along Enfield Street, which is Route 5, and it is made up mostly of houses from the 1700s to the early 1900s. Plumbing in a house that has been updated many times starts with finding out what is actually in the walls.

What the historic district is

The Enfield Historic District was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1979. It covers about 160 acres and runs along Enfield Street from Route 190 in the north to the junction of Old King Street and Oliver Road in the south, with the town common at its center.

Residential buildings dominate, mostly wood-frame houses of 1.5 to 2.5 stories, in styles that include Georgian, Greek Revival and Late Victorian. Civic buildings include an 1848 Congregational Church and the former town hall.

Why an old house needs a careful first look

A house that has stood for a century or two has usually had its plumbing and heating changed more than once. That does not tell us what is inside, because one house may have been fully updated while its neighbor kept older work. We do not assume a material from a date.

We open an access point, camera a drain or trace a supply line, and then explain what we find in plain terms. If a repair would disturb finished plaster or old floors, we say so and plan around it.

Permits and approvals for older homes

Plumbing work in Connecticut follows the 2022 Connecticut State Building Code, which adopts the 2021 International Plumbing Code (IPC), and some jobs need a permit. Ask the Enfield building office what your project requires before work begins.

National Register listing is a recognition of the district. Whether any local review applies to your exterior work is a question for the town. We handle the plumbing, and we explain when a step needs the building office.
