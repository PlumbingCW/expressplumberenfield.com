Sump Pump System Installation in Enfield, CT: How It Is Done

A sump pump system collects groundwater in a pit and pumps it out of the house before it can flood the basement. It is built from a pit, a pump, a check valve, a discharge line and, ideally, a backup. Each part has a job, and a weak one can defeat the rest.

This guide explains how installation is done, how to choose the pump, what backups are available and what to ask before you hire a contractor in the Enfield area.

The parts of a sump system

The pit, or basin, is a hole in the floor lined with a plastic or concrete liner, deep enough for the pump to sit and for water to collect. The pump sits in the pit and switches on when a float lifts. A discharge pipe carries the water out of the house, and a check valve in that pipe stops the water falling back into the pit.

A sealed lid keeps debris and radon-bearing air out of the basement. A battery or water-powered backup runs the pump when the power fails or the main pump dies. An alarm can warn you when the water rises too high.

How an installation is done

01

Locate the pit

We find the low spot where water collects, or the point where an interior drain will end. The pit goes there, away from footings and utilities.

02

Cut the slab and dig the pit

We cut a hole in the concrete, dig to the needed depth, set the liner and place gravel at the bottom so water can enter.

03

Set the pump and the check valve

The pump goes into the liner and the discharge pipe goes up through the check valve. The float must move freely and not touch the wall.

04

Run the discharge

The pipe leaves the house and ends where water can drain away from the foundation. It should slope and be protected from freezing and from being blocked.

05

Wire, test and seal

The pump plugs into a dedicated GFCI outlet. We fill the pit with water, test the float and the valve and seal the lid.

The discharge line decides whether the system works

Water pumped out of the house has to go somewhere that is not back toward the foundation. A discharge that ends a few feet from the wall can seep back into the soil and into the pit, so the pump runs again and again. The pipe should carry the water well away, to a slope, a dry well or a drain where it is allowed.

In a freeze, the discharge can ice over and block. A pipe that slopes down and ends with an open or relief outlet reduces the risk. We also avoid sending the water to a neighbor's property or to the sanitary sewer, which is not allowed in most places.

Backup options worth considering

A pump alone is not protection during a storm outage.

  • <strong>Battery backup.</strong> A second pump runs on a battery when power fails, for hours, depending on use.
  • <strong>Water-powered backup.</strong> It uses water pressure and works as long as the supply holds.
  • <strong>Generator.</strong> A standby or portable generator can run the pump and other loads.
  • <strong>High-water alarm.</strong> It warns you before the pit overflows.
  • <strong>Regular testing.</strong> Pour water into the pit and make sure it runs, a few times a year.

What to ask a contractor

Ask what pump and basin they will install, and why. Ask where the discharge will go and how it avoids freezing. Ask about a backup, and what it costs. Ask whether the electrical work is included, and who handles any permit. Get the scope in writing, with the price for the pump, the pit, the discharge and the finishing.

A fair contractor looks at the water problem first and explains if the pump alone will do or if a drain system is needed. For repairs and replacements, see sump pump repair, and for the larger picture, basement waterproofing.

Maintenance that keeps the system alive

Test the pump every few months and before the wet season. Clean the intake screen if the pump has one, and make sure the float moves freely. Check the discharge outlet outside for blockage. Replace the backup battery as the maker advises, and keep the lid sealed.

A pump that runs constantly or cycles often is a sign of too much water, a stuck float or an undersized pump. Do not ignore it. A pump that works hard in dry weather may be pumping the same water around, and a plumber can find the cause.

Electrical and safety details people forget

A sump pump should have its own GFCI-protected outlet, not a shared extension cord. Plug it in where water cannot reach the outlet, and avoid running the cord across the wet floor. If the pump needs a hardwired circuit, an electrician should do it.

Make sure the lid is secure, because an open pit is a hazard for children and pets. Label the breaker so anyone can find it in an emergency, and keep a flashlight near the pit. Test the alarm and the backup together, so you know the whole system works.

Code and electrical notes

Discharge piping follows the 2021 International Plumbing Code (IPC), which the 2022 Connecticut State Building Code adopts, and the outlet and wiring follow electrical code. Never run a cord through standing water. The Connecticut Department of Consumer Protection licenses plumbers, so you can ask any plumber about their license before work starts.
